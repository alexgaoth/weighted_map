The United States, redrawn by driving time

A road map is drawn from latitude and longitude, so the distance between two places is the distance a crow would fly. Nobody drives like a crow. This map keeps every county's true compass bearing from a fixed origin and throws its distance away, putting it back at the distance you could have driven in the time the trip actually takes — one hour to a hundred kilometres.

What the map actually does

Pick an origin. For every county seat in the contiguous 48 states, ask a routing engine how long the drive takes. Keep the bearing from the origin exactly as it is, then move the county to a new distance: driving hours × 100 km. A place four hours away lands 400 km out, whether it is 200 km or 380 km away in reality.

All of this happens in an azimuthal equidistant projection centred on the origin, where straight-line distance from the centre is true geodesic distance and the polar angle is true azimuth. In that plane the transformation is exactly the sentence above: keep θ, replace r. The rings on the maps are therefore both distances and durations — the 10-hour ring sits at 1,000 km.

The scale is not arbitrary in the way it first looks. Across all 6,218 routes the median implied door-to-door speed is 101.4 km/h. One hour to a hundred kilometres is very nearly the speed of the American road network, which means the map's baseline is "an average trip" and every departure from it is a place that is faster or slower than typical.

Water bends this map further than mountains do

This is where the American result parts company with the Chinese one. In China the great distortions are Tibet, Xinjiang and the northern Northeast: high, remote, hard to build roads through. The United States has mountains too, but the interstate system crosses them at speed. What the American map cannot absorb is water, and the ridge-and-valley grain of the Appalachians.

Nine of the top fifteen are the same two obstacles. Northampton and Accomack counties sit on the Virginia Eastern Shore: 194 km from the Capitol as the crow flies, 327 km by road, because the only way there is around the top of the Chesapeake or over the Bay Bridge–Tunnel. Mathews and Gloucester are on the necks below it, Cape May is across Delaware Bay, and Worcester is on the far side of Delmarva. The Chesapeake is doing to the mid-Atlantic what the Himalaya does to western China, on a smaller scale and for the same reason: you cannot drive across it.

The rest — Potter, Clinton, Mifflin, Centre, Cameron, Juniata — are all in the same place, the ridge-and-valley Appalachians of central Pennsylvania. The ridges run southwest–northeast in long parallel walls, and the roads run in the valleys between them. Going northwest from Washington means crossing the grain, and there is no straight way to do it.

The most stretched place in the country reachable by car, Nantucket, is 646 km from Washington and 10.5 hours away — a 1.62× penalty imposed almost entirely by a ferry timetable.

Stretch is circuity times slowness

A county gets pushed outward for exactly two reasons, and they multiply. Either the road bends — it is longer than the straight line — or the road is slow. Written out, stretch = (road ÷ straight) × (100 ÷ km/h), so the two causes can be separated and mapped independently.

{{PLATE:why:5:The two causes, separated}}

Splitting the variance of log stretch between the two terms gives a clean answer that changes with the origin. From the Capitol the split is 51% circuity, 49% slowness — an even contest. From the geographic centre it is 68% circuity, 32% slowness, because most trips out of Kansas begin on the Plains grid, where the roads are fast but refuse to go diagonally. Driving from Lebanon to Dighton, Kansas means 221 km of straight line and 319 km of road: a detour factor of 1.44, which is essentially √2. That is not terrain. That is the Public Land Survey System, drawn in 1785, still charging a toll.

The emptiest roads are the truest

The counties that barely move are all in the same kind of place. From the Capitol the ten least stretched are in west Texas, Nevada and Wyoming — Van Horn, Sierra Blanca, Pecos, Battle Mountain, Evanston — at a stretch of 1.03 to 1.04 and implied speeds of 107 to 109 km/h over journeys of 2,500 to 3,400 km. Interstate 10 through the Trans-Pecos is posted at 80 mph. You really can average 108 km/h from Washington D.C. to Van Horn, Texas, over more than a day of driving, and on this map Van Horn therefore sits almost exactly where the atlas puts it.

This is the inversion of the Chinese result. There, the periphery is where the map tears: remote means slow. In the United States, remote means fast. Nevada is the least distorted state from both origins.

The distortion belongs to the origin, not the terrain

Running the same construction from two places makes something obvious that one map alone hides: a state's stretch is not a property of that state. It is a property of the route.

Pennsylvania is the third most distorted state in the country seen from Washington and the second least distorted seen from Kansas. Nothing about Pennsylvania changed. From Washington you enter it across the ridges at short range, and the crossing is the whole trip. From Kansas you arrive along I-70 or I-80 after 1,800 km of open highway, and the same ridges are a rounding error. Arizona and New Mexico run the other way: unremarkable from Washington, badly stretched from Kansas, because the straight line from the Plains to the Southwest runs into the Rockies and the Colorado Plateau and the road has to go around.

Carrying the borders along

Moving 3,109 points is easy. Making the coastline and the state lines follow them is the part that requires a choice, because between the county seats there is no measurement — and over the Great Lakes, out past Cape Cod, and beyond the border there is no land either. Three interpolators were fitted to the same displacement data:

  • i

    Radial stretch field. Interpolate the log of the stretch factor across the plane, then move every boundary vertex along its own bearing by that factor. Guarantees the warp stays purely radial, which is what the concept actually claims.

  • ii

    Piecewise affine. Delaunay-triangulate the county seats and let each triangle carry its own affine map — the classic triangulation deformation.

  • iii

    Thin-plate spline. A smooth radial-basis fit to the displacement vectors, minimising bending energy; the topologically gentle option.

All three reproduce the 3,109 measured positions to within 0.000 m, which is the point: on the data they are identical, and any visible disagreement is the extrapolation showing itself.

How it was built, and where it is soft

County seats came from Wikidata keyed on FIPS codes (3,049 of 3,109; the remaining 60, mostly Virginia independent cities that are their own seat, use the Census internal point). Boundaries are Census cartographic files at 1:5,000,000, densified to 10 km segments so that straight state lines can bend. Routing is OSRM's public server on the OpenStreetMap network, one source against 99 destinations per request.

OSRM's default car profile is conservative, so the raw times were calibrated against Valhalla — an independent engine on the same road data — over 224 routes sampled around ten anchors from Boston to Bakersfield. The ratio came out at 1.187 ± 0.076, flat across distance bands (1.176 / 1.191 / 1.184 for 0–100, 100–200 and 200–300 km) and stable across regions, so a single scalar correction is defensible. Every time on this page is a raw OSRM duration divided by 1.187.
